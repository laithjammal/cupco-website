The business

What Cupco actually sells

Cupco manufactures custom-printed paper coffee cups in Sydney and sells them to small hospitality businesses. The site is a four-page Wix build: a homepage, a shop with four blank-stock SKUs, and policy pages. Everything custom happens by enquiry.

The offer as it stands

  • 1 box minimum — one carton, 1,000 cups (50/bag, 20 bags/carton)
  • 10-day lead time, framed on the hero as “Ready Next Week”
  • Australian made, Sydney
  • Artwork support — “our design team works with you to bring your unique brand to life”
  • Quote within 24 hours

The six pillars Cupco already claims

Your Design · Low Order Quantities · 10 Day Lead-time · Premium Quality · Aussie Made · Unbeatable Price. These are the right six territories. Five of them are keepers. One of them — Low Order Quantities — is the weakest card in the deck, and section 03 explains why.

02

Audit

Where the current site leaks

Ranked by how much conversion each one costs. The first three are the ones that matter; the rest are cumulative friction.

01

No custom price. Anywhere.

Critical

Every competitor publishes a “from $X” figure. Cupco asks a café owner to hand over their phone number to learn a number the market already advertises. This is the single biggest drop-off on the page, and fixing it is the whole thesis of the new page.

02

The process section is unfinished

Critical

“Our Process” renders step 1 — Design — and then stops. There is no step 2, 3 or 4. A visitor deciding whether to trust a stranger with $500 is looking at an abandoned page.

03

Policy pages are unedited Wix placeholder text

Critical

The shipping policy currently reads “a Shipping Policy often addresses these types of issues…”. Freight cost and delivery time are precisely the two unknowns this buyer is anxious about, and the page that should answer them is boilerplate. Same for refunds.

04

The search snippet promises eco, the page delivers none

High

The meta description says Cupco makes cups “that are environmentally friendly.” There is not one word about materials, lining, compostability or certification on the page. People who click that promise bounce.

05

No sizes, no wall types, no lid compatibility

High

8oz / 12oz / 16oz, single vs double wall, which lid fits which cup. A café owner cannot picture the order, so they can’t place it.

06

Thin social proof

High

Three testimonials, first name and initial only. No business names, no cup photos, no Google rating, no ABN, no count of cafés served. A rival leads with “Rated 4.8 stars on Google” and shows its ABN in the footer.

07

No sample offer

High

The lowest-friction yes in this category, and Cupco has nothing. Competitors ship sample packs within 24 hours and make it a headline CTA. A sample request is a qualified lead with a delivery address attached.

08

One conversion path only

Medium

The form is the only real ask. The phone number sits in the header and the footer but is never a button, never sticky on mobile, and never repeated beside a reason to call. Email is display text, not a link.

09

No risk reversal

Medium

Nothing tells the buyer what happens if the print colour comes out wrong. The obvious, free, honest guarantee — we don’t print until you approve the proof — is missing.

10

Typo in a testimonial

Minor

“Fast turnaround, great service and surprisingly welly priced.” On a page whose job is to signal that this manufacturer is careful with detail.

The uncomfortable finding

“Low Order Quantities” is Cupco’s weakest claim, not its strongest. Rivals go down to 500, and some of the wider market advertises 250. A café owner who shops around will find a lower minimum within two clicks. Leading with it invites the one comparison Cupco loses.

The reframe

At 1,000 cups the competition sits around $0.85–$0.94 per cup. Cupco’s blank stock at $60 per 1,000 suggests real cost headroom. If a printed box lands anywhere near $0.45–$0.55 per cup, the story writes itself — and it is a story nobody else in this market is telling, because they’re all anchored on a 500-unit price that looks small and reads expensive per cup.

So the box stops being an apology for a minimum and becomes the proposition: one box is all you have to store, and all you have to pay for. Same fact, opposite framing. It moves Cupco off the axis it loses on and onto the one it wins.

Positioning line for the page

Lead with price per cup, back it with Australian made, and use one box as the practical proof that it fits a small café — not as the headline benefit.

The offer

Sharpened

Core promise: Your brand on your cups, one box at a time, printed in Sydney, on your counter in ten days.

Conversion paths, in priority order

  1. Get my price

    The form. Primary CTA everywhere on the page. Never labelled “Submit”.

  2. Call 0416 888 639

    Click-to-call, sticky on mobile. This buyer converts by phone more than any web form predicts.

  3. Send a free sample pack

    The micro-conversion. Cups in hand, posted same day. Lowest-friction yes on the page and a qualified lead with an address.

  4. Email Info@cupco.com.au

    A real mailto link for the people who’d rather attach their artwork than fill anything in.

Risk reversal — the three promises

  • Proof before print. Nothing goes on press until you’ve seen it and said yes. Costs Cupco nothing, removes the biggest fear.
  • A quote in 24 hours. Already claimed on the site. Put it beside the button, not in a corner.
  • Artwork setup included. Turns “I don’t have print-ready files” from a blocker into a reason to choose Cupco.

Every one of these is free to offer and already true. None require a new business decision — only that Cupco is willing to put them in writing.

08

Lead capture

The form

The current form asks for first name, last name, phone, email, business name and a message — six fields for someone who just wants a price. Every field is a chance to leave.

Recommended structure

  • Two chip rows above the fields — cup size and rough quantity, tappable, nothing typed. They’re a commitment device: choosing gets people invested before they’ve given up anything.
  • Name — one field, not two
  • Best number to reach you — the priority field, phone keypad on mobile
  • Email
  • Business name — qualifies the lead at no cost
  • Anything else? — optional, clearly marked, collapsed by default

Details that move the number

  • Button reads “Get my price”, never “Submit”
  • Under the button: “We’ll come back within 24 hours. No obligation.”
  • Inline validation on blur, never a wall of red on submit
  • No captcha — use a honeypot field instead
  • The thank-you state does work: what happens next, the phone number for anyone impatient, and the sample-pack offer as a second yes
  • Route submissions to a phone as well as an inbox. A 24-hour promise fails if it’s waiting on someone opening a laptop.

The sample form is separate, and shorter

Name, business, delivery address. Three fields. It converts people who aren’t ready to talk about money yet — and it puts a Cupco cup in the hand of someone who is about to compare it with a competitor’s.

09

Mechanics

Rules the build has to hold to

Mobile is the page

  • Above the fold: headline, one line of sub, primary CTA, phone. Nothing else competes.
  • A sticky bottom bar carrying Call and Get my price from first scroll to last.
  • Tap targets 44px minimum. Owner-operators are doing this one-handed during service.

CTA discipline

  • The primary CTA repeats roughly every one and a half screens.
  • It uses the same three words every time. Varied CTA labels read as a different offer each time.
  • Trust sits next to the button — the 24-hour promise, the proof guarantee — not in a distant section.

Speed

  • Target LCP under 2.5s on 4G. The current homepage ships over 1.1MB of HTML before a single image.
  • Cup photography compressed hard and lazy-loaded below the fold; hero image preloaded.
  • If this page runs on Wix, budget for that ceiling. If it can be a standalone build, take it.

Fix the foundations at the same time

  • Write real shipping and refund policies. They’re linked from the footer and they’re currently placeholder text.
  • Correct the “welly priced” testimonial.
  • Match the meta description to the page, or add the eco content it promises.

Measurement

How we’ll know it worked

Targets. For warm paid traffic to a page this specific, 4–8% of visitors becoming leads is a reasonable band; below 2% means the offer or the price isn’t landing. Track quote-to-order separately — a page that lifts leads while dropping order rate is attracting the wrong traffic, not converting better.

Use a call-tracking number. Without one, every phone lead is attributed to nothing, and the page will look like it’s underperforming while the phone rings.
